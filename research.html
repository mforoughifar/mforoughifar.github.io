The Challenges of Deploying an Algorithmic Pricing Tool: Evidence from Airbnb

with Nitin Mehta

The question. Airbnb built Smart Pricing, an AI tool that sets nightly prices for hosts. Most hosts never turned it on. Why would people refuse free help with a hard problem?

What we found. Using listing data from four U.S. cities and a dynamic structural model of hosts’ pricing and adoption decisions, we show the main barrier was belief, not performance: hosts expected the algorithm to do worse than it actually did. Hosts who rarely adjust their own prices would have gained the most, yet they were the least likely to adopt. Correcting those beliefs, and retraining the algorithm on hosts’ true costs (which the platform cannot observe directly), would raise profits for both hosts and Airbnb.

Building a good algorithm is half the job. Getting people to trust it is the other half.

The Impact of Word of Mouth Precision and Consumer Sophistication on Firm Pricing and Profit

with David Soberman

The question. Shoppers learn about quality from reviews, testimonials, and friends. Some of that information is accurate and some is noise, and shoppers can’t always tell which is which. How should a firm price when that is true?

What we found. In a game-theoretic model, more accurate word of mouth is not always good news for a high-quality firm, and smarter consumers are not always better customers. Price works as a signal of quality only under a narrow set of conditions; otherwise the firm lets word of mouth do the talking.

Better information can leave the better product worse off. Theory papers exist to ruin intuitions like that.

Barriers to AI Inputs: Implications for Competition and Innovation

with Lee Tiedrich and Jimmy Royer

The question. To compete with AI, a firm needs data, models, computing power, and people who know what to do with them. What stands between an ordinary firm and those ingredients?

What we found. We map the barriers to each input and the practical ways around them, such as infrastructure-as-a-service and licensing commercially available AI tools, and we lay out what policymakers, industry, researchers, and data rights holders can each do to keep AI markets open and trustworthy.

Less Traffic, More Attention? A Field Experiment on Publisher AI Summaries

with Yichen Pan and Param Vir Singh

The question. Publishers are putting AI-written summaries at the top of their own articles. Does that help readers, or does it give them a reason not to click?

What we found. Working with a large sports media publisher, we randomly assigned 31,253 articles to show an AI summary or not. For newly published articles, which generate nearly all of the publisher’s ad revenue, summaries cut article views by 24.7% while raising the average reader’s engagement time by 7.5%. The net effect was a 23.8% drop in ad revenue from those articles. Most of the lost traffic came early in an article’s life and from reduced exposure in organic search.

−24.7% article views +7.5% engagement time −23.8% ad revenue 8.1M page views 56M ad impressions

The readers who stay pay more attention. There are just fewer of them.

Improving Educational Technology with Collaborative Design

with Anuj Kumar

The question. Most learning software treats each student as an individual. What if the product were built around teams instead?

What we found. We built an EdTech platform and ran a randomized field experiment across 63 math classes (grades 3–6) in 12 schools in India. Classes were assigned either an individual design, with personal feedback and recognition, or a team design that shares knowledge gaps within groups and rewards the team. The team design narrowed achievement gaps within classrooms and raised skills for every type of student, including the strongest. Nudges and incentives to complete assignments were roughly four times more effective under the team design.

63 classes 12 schools ≈4× nudge effectiveness

Working paper 2025

The Intended and Unintended Consequences of Short-Term Rental Policies on Home-Sharing Platforms: Evidence from Airbnb

with Unnati Narang

The question. Cities regulate short-term rentals with everything from licenses to outright bans, mostly to push out commercial operators. Most studies look at one city at a time. What happens when you compare them all?

What we found. Across 42 cities, short-term rental policies reduced Airbnb listings by 11.87% and unique hosts by 10.81% on average. The type of rule matters: complete bans cut listings by 20.26%, availability caps by 11.52%, and licensing requirements by 8.04%, while geographic restrictions had no significant effect. We also document side effects the policies did not intend, including lower service quality among hosts who stay.

42 cities −11.9% listings on average −20.3% under bans

“Regulate Airbnb” is not one policy. It is at least four, and they do different things.

Working paper 2025

Communicating Uncertainty Can Increase AI Adoption

with Rozhina Ghanavi, Ryan Webb, and Avi Goldfarb

The question. AI tools usually present recommendations with total confidence. Would people follow them more, or less, if the AI admitted what it doesn’t know?

What we found. In randomized experiments where people set short-term rental prices with help from an AI, showing uncertainty made people follow the recommendation more closely. Two kinds of uncertainty matter. Showing the predicted chance of a booking at the recommended price clarifies the risk of following the AI; showing a confidence range around the price changes how precise the advice feels. Adherence was highest when both were shown, especially when feedback was scarce or the market was volatile.

An AI that says “I’m about 70% sure” gets listened to more than one that pretends to be certain. This also works for professors.
