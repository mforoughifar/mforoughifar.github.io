Marketing academic job market tips

The job market is survivable. Here’s the whole playbook.

In 2022 I went through the quantitative marketing job market. Afterwards I wrote down everything I wish someone had told me, as a series of LinkedIn posts for Ph.D. students. The full series now lives here, in order, from submitting your materials to what to do if the market doesn’t work out. Side effects of the job market may include dreaming in slide transitions.

If you only read one section

My three most important tips.

1

Practice your talk until you are sick of it. Then practice more.

Present as many times as you can, to different faculty members. Mock interviews and mock job talks are essential. Aim for at least 10 to 20 of them.

Your advisor has seen the talk nine times and can no longer tell whether slide 14 makes sense. Find fresh victims.

2

Write down every question you get, and prepare an answer to each.

Keep a running log of the questions from every mock and every real interview, and think through a careful answer for each one. You are ready when a talk ends and no question was new. My own document ended up with 150 to 200 unique questions.

There are only about forty questions in the world. Thirty-eight of them are “what about endogeneity?” in different outfits.

3

Do your homework on the school, and show up excited.

Look up the faculty at every school you interview with. Knowing your audience gives you an advantage in every conversation. And when you talk about your research, be energetic and confident. If you don’t sound excited about your paper, nobody else will be.

Try tip 2 right now

Start your question log.

After every mock talk and every interview, type in each question you were asked. Before the next one, reread the list and make sure you have a good answer to each.

You are ready when you finish a talk and have nothing new to add.

The list is saved only in your own browser. Nothing is uploaded. Use “Save as text file” to keep a copy.

    #1 Summer · before deadlines Original post, July 2025 →

    Submitting your materials

    I’m starting a series of posts for PhD students on the quant marketing job market this year. When I went through the process in summer 2022, I was surprised by how little detailed guidance was available, especially from people who’d recently been through it. So, I’m sharing the kind of info I wish I had seen.

    How my job market timeline looked

    What to do right now

    Get your core documents ready.

    • A polished Job Market Paper
    • CV, cover letter
    • Research, teaching, and diversity statements
    • Three letters of recommendation (rarely 4)
    • Some schools may ask for a second paper (rarely more), so be prepared.

    Set up a website. Nothing fancy—just professional and clean, with easy access to your materials.

    Look the part. Buy a decent suit or outfit. You’ll need it for interviews—yes, even the virtual ones.

    Upgrade your tech setup. Most first-round interviews will be on Zoom. If your webcam or mic isn’t great, invest in better ones. And use a real background if possible—it makes a difference.

    Expect chaos and plan for it.

    True story

    This was my reality:

    • A fire alarm went off 20 min before one interview
    • Zoom crashed during my first interview with an Asian school (which was at night)
    • A mailman showed up mid-interview and refused to leave
    • Zoom updates caused audio/screen issues more than once

    Restart your laptop before every interview. Zoom updates can break things, and a reboot often solves it.

    Letters of rec: ask early. A few weeks before the first deadline is usually fine—but don’t assume everything will go smoothly. Some schools reached out to me for interviews before letters were in—and only asked for them afterward.

    Apply early. This one matters: don’t wait for the deadline. In my year, several schools started contacting candidates before their official deadlines. If your materials are ready, hit submit.

    Bottom line You’ve worked for years to reach this stage. Now is the time to set the tone for how you show up on the market. Polish your materials, apply early, and don’t let tech or logistics trip you up.

    #2 Interview invites start Original post, July 2025 →

    Preparing for first-round interviews

    It’s normal to feel like application prep and submission is taking forever. It does. For everyone.

    Tip #1: Practice. Practice. Practice.

    Refine your JMP pitch with faculty and PhD students. Gather all questions you’re asked. Keep a doc. Sharpen answers. I changed parts of my model after questions I couldn’t answer. Yup—I rewrote my structural model mid-market.

    My doc had 150–200 unique questions. Once you hit that, questions start repeating. Reread the doc before each interview.

    Who gets interviews? It’s a signaling game

    • Your JMP must stand out.
    • CV + pipeline matter.
    • Publications or R&Rs help.
    • School brand helps. Rotman opened doors for me.
    • Advisor’s role is huge—active promotion (emails, calls) makes a real difference.
    • Empirical/quant work is more in demand in many schools vs. analytical work. Structural vs. reduced-form vs. ML preferences vary by place.

    First-round interviews

    • 15–45 min on Zoom
    • 2–10 faculty
    • Focused on your JMP

    How to answer questions

    • Short and to the point
    • Thank every person that asks a question
    • Somebody told me in an interview I shouldn’t say “that’s a good question” to every question

    You may not be able to finish your talk. You will be interrupted.

    • My slide rule: 1 slide per 1 min, max.
    • Have hyperlinks to skip to results.
    True story

    At CMU, I couldn’t get to the main results (too many questions)—clicked the hyperlink and jumped to main results in last 60 secs. Thought I blew it.

    Reader, they hired him.

    I bombed my first 2 interviews (both top schools). It’s OK, it’s learning by doing. Wish I could’ve scheduled the more important schools later.

    Don’t ignore tech. Zoom crashes, unstable internet, etc.—they happen. Have a plan.

    “Do you have any questions for us?”

    Never say no. Always start by expressing interest. Then ask about:

    • Teaching expectations
    • Research support
    • Timeline
    • Internal seminars, collaboration, etc.

    Have an answer for: “why did you apply to our school?”

    Always send a thank-you email. Brief, friendly, sincere.

    My conversion from interview to flyout was ~40%. That’s higher than average. But honestly—some “great” interviews led nowhere, and some “meh” ones got me flyouts. Don’t overanalyze outcomes.

    Final takeaway You can’t control who shortlists you. But once you’re in the Zoom room, preparation is everything. Practice your pitch. Know your paper. Stay cool. Every interview could be the one.

    #2.1 Interview season Original post, July 2025 →

    Interview season prep: more personal experience

    If you’ve started getting interview invites—congrats! You’re officially in the most intense stretch of the job market. Here is practical advice from my first-round interviews.

    Mock interviews (do a LOT of them)

    • If you have 20 faculty + 10 students in your department, that’s ~10 small mock sessions with 2–4 people each.
    • Ask a few to play tough interviewers to simulate pressure.
    • Every question you get in a real interview should ideally be something you’ve already heard, written down, and practiced.

    When you get a new question in a real interview

    • If you can, answer it concisely on the spot.
    • If not, stay calm. Be honest. Say: “That’s a great point—I’ll think more about it and would be happy to follow up.”
    • Then actually think it through. You’ll likely get that question again in other interviews or in the flyout with the same school (if you end up getting it).

    Be ready to defend your assumptions

    Every paper has assumptions. Interviewers will challenge them. Be ready to explain why you made each one—and what supports it. Across all my interviews, I literally was challenged for EVERY assumption in my structural model (even on the standard deviation of the error term).

    Identification is critical—especially in quant interviews

    True story

    I was grilled once for 10 minutes on the IV in my model. They asked whether it really satisfies the exclusion restriction and proposed scenarios where it might fail.

    Before each interview

    • Skim CVs of attendees, if shared.
    • Be ready for interviews without slides (yes, it happens).
    • One top school asked for NO SLIDES but a 2–3 min verbal summary, then jumped into questioning model assumptions—even though only some had read the paper. It was challenging but fun.

    Slide tips (non-obvious but game-changing)

    • Don’t paste full sentences from your paper.
    • Cut 3-line bullets into short phrases.
    • Use animations to reveal bullet points 1 by 1—helps focus.
    • Slide titles = takeaways, not labels.
    • Highlight key numbers in tables.
    • Build for mixed audiences (Quant + CB)—I rarely showed equations at all.

    How you talk matters more than you think

    • Show energy—smile, vary your tone.
    • Don’t speak in a monotone. Emphasize what matters.

    Handling tough questions

    • Keep answers short. Don’t ramble.
    • Don’t get flustered if interrupted.
    • Some schools will flood you with questions to test your composure. Stay cool, stay clear.

    Final takeaway First-round interviews are rarely clean or predictable. Your job: stay calm, know your paper deeply, and show genuine excitement. A thoughtful answer often beats a perfect slide.

    #3.1 Flyouts Original post, August 2025 →

    Flyouts: what to expect and how to prepare

    If you’re at the flyout stage—congrats! This is where schools decide if you’re not just a great researcher, but also a future colleague they want to work with. I had 15 flyouts in my 2022 market season, and here’s what I learned.

    1. Logistics matter

    • Don’t forget key items—suit, shoes, USB, laptop, slides.
    • Have backups—extra clicker batteries, a copy of your talk on a USB.
    True story

    I once forgot my shoes for a flyout.

    2. The schedule is intense

    A typical day:

    • Job talk
    • Back-to-back 1-on-1 meetings
    • Lunch with faculty or students
    • Dinner with a smaller group

    By the end, you’ll have met 10–15 people. It’s a long day—keep water and a snack handy to maintain energy.

    3. The job talk is again central—practice, practice, practice

    • Do the same practices as for online interviews (multiple mock talks) but this time in a longer format (60–90 min).
    • Show interest in the audience questions. Be articulate, to the point, and patient in answering.
    • Once I said “If you allow me to continue…” and they stopped asking questions completely.
    • Expect different styles: some schools pepper you with questions from the start, others let you talk a bit first.

    4. 1-on-1 meetings can make or break you

    • These are not filler—they’re a big part of how people decide if you’d be a good fit.
    • Come prepared with questions for them, not just answers about yourself.
    • If they lead the conversation, great. If not, have a list of questions: teaching load, mentoring programs, research support, housing, quality of life in the city.
    • Avoid highlighting weaknesses (“Do you have blah?” if you know they don’t).

    5. Do your homework

    • Read recent paper abstracts by people you’re meeting—especially if their work overlaps with yours.
    • If they haven’t published recently, don’t ask about old work. Instead: “What are you working on these days?”
    • Showing genuine interest in their research goes a long way.

    6. Prepare for repeat questions

    • Questions you couldn’t answer in the first round will come back in the flyout.
    • Have clear, concise answers ready—bonus points if you updated your paper or analysis in response.

    7. Personal questions happen (even though they shouldn’t)

    • You might be asked about your relationship status or location preferences.
    • If you like them, signal it clearly. If you’re open, just say you’re open.
    • If you already have offers from other schools, be strategic in how you discuss competing interest.

    Final takeaway Flyouts are as much about fit and personality as they are about research. People want to see you present well and imagine you as a colleague. Be prepared and personable. Keep your energy up.

    #3.2 Flyouts Original post, September 2025 →

    Flyouts: the job talk and beyond

    At the flyout stage, your job talk is the centerpiece. Departments are asking: “Can this person explain their research clearly, defend it under pressure, and excite us about their agenda?” In my year (2022), each flyout was different, but a few lessons stood out.

    Presentation tips from experience

    1. The first slides are critical. If you don’t motivate your research question quickly, you’ll struggle to keep attention later. Faculty want to know why your problem matters before they care how you solved it. So, the first three slides should be absolutely perfect.
    2. For structural models: start with reduced-form evidence. One thing that worked really well for me: after each reduced-form result, I would connect it directly to a structural assumption that would appear later. By the time I got to the model, the audience already understood most assumptions without seeing equations. It took time to make this coherent, but when it worked, it landed very well.
    3. Be ready to defend every assumption. You can’t show reduced-form evidence for all structural model assumptions, but across 15 flyouts, every single assumption in my model was challenged—sometimes by Quant folks, sometimes CB or Strategy. Don’t skip this prep.
    4. Identification will come up. At top quant schools, someone will ask. Best practice: start with a clear verbal explanation that maps data variation to the parameter you’re estimating. Many will be satisfied with this. If not, have backup slides for details.
    5. Backup slides: use wisely. Have them, but avoid flipping back and forth—it eats time and breaks flow. Answer verbally whenever possible.
    6. Managing questions is an art. Sometimes you’ll be bombarded with questions early. If you can give quick answers, do it. Otherwise, politely postpone some answers to future slides (and come back directly to the person who asked). This shows confidence and keeps you on track.
    7. Always be ready to skip. I never finished all my slides. Over time, I learned which ones were expendable. I built “magic links” into my deck so I could jump smoothly to results, instead of awkwardly scrolling through 10 slides.

    Miscellaneous but important

    • Sleep well the night before. It sounds obvious, but sharpness matters.
    • Research the people you’ll meet 1-on-1. Start by asking what they’re working on now.
    • Show genuine energy and interest in the school. Flyouts are about fit as much as research.

    Final takeaway In a flyout, you’re not just presenting research—you’re showing how you think on your feet, how you engage under pressure, and what kind of colleague you’ll be. Prepare deeply, manage the room, and don’t forget to show your excitement.

    #4 Offer in hand Original post, October 2025 →

    Offers and negotiations

    If you have an offer in hand, congrats—this is the best part of the job market.

    When I was on the market in 2022, my first offer arrived in mid-October while I still had many flyouts left. I told the school I wanted to finish all flyouts before deciding, and they were fine. Eventually, I received multiple offers and accepted the last one, which came in late November.

    1. How offers arrive

    Usually, the dean or associate dean calls with a verbal offer, summarizing:

    • Base salary (9-month + possible 2/9 summer support)
    • Research budget
    • Teaching load
    • Tenure clock length
    • Moving expenses

    Sometimes a formal letter follows, other times it comes only after verbal acceptance.

    2. Timelines vary

    • Most top schools are flexible and rarely give exploding offers.
    • Mid or lower-tier schools sometimes give 1–2 weeks to decide. It’s not ideal for candidates, but it happens.
    • Often, a few top candidates get most offers. If top schools could move faster, others would have more opportunities—but that’s not always the case.
    • Always ask politely for more time if you need it—it’s expected.

    3. Don’t rush decisions

    • I’ve seen great candidates take the first offer because it’s the most money they’ve ever seen. That’s not always wise. Consult your advisers.
    • If you have multiple offers, don’t rush. Think about research fit, teaching expectations, city, and colleagues—not just salary.

    4. Negotiation is normal

    • Don’t be shy. Schools expect it, especially if you have multiple offers.
    • You can ask for more salary (easier if you have a competing offer). And always fight for more research budget.
    • I had competing offers and negotiated two rounds, improving both salary and research support.
    A line that works

    When making a final ask, be clear it’s the last one: “If you could meet this adjustment, I’d be very happy to accept.” It’s polite, firm, and signals closure.

    5. Handling multiple or exploding offers

    • I was told to avoid holding more than 2–3 offers—it’s inefficient and schools talk.
    • If you get an exploding deadline, explain you’re completing other interviews and request an extension. Some schools cooperate if you communicate well.

    6. Thinking about family and life

    If you have a partner or family, mention it after the offer. Schools often help with spousal job searches, but those take time.

    7. Declining an offer gracefully

    Be respectful and brief. Thank them for the opportunity, time, and flexibility. Academia is small—you’ll meet again.

    Final takeaway This is where you finally have some power. Use it wisely. Be professional and assertive, and remember—it’s fine to ask for more. You’ve worked years for this. Choose the offer that fits your long-term goals, not just short-term relief.

    If you’re on the market with no offers yet—hang in there. Even star candidates can have only one job. Hopefully your turn will come soon.

    #5 Still searching Original post, December 2025 →

    After the market

    By now many schools have finished hiring and many candidates have accepted offers—congrats. This post is for those still searching. You’re not alone. I know this stage is frustrating. I was in a similar boat: I had accepted a job but couldn’t join, so I was back on the market.

    This year is tough. I’ve seen strong people end up with no offer. That’s not a verdict on your ability. There are other paths forward (not exclusive).

    1. The secondary academic market

    • Late reopened searches
    • Schools that didn’t fill
    • Late hires, visiting roles, short contracts

    What to do:

    • Tell people you’re available and flexible (visiting, location).
    • Recontact places where you interviewed or were “close.” Example: I declined an offer, told friends, they reached out, and they got offers.
    • Treat this phase like a revision: tighten the story, add robustness, clean the draft.

    This isn’t failure—often a delay.

    2. Industry is not second best (and they don’t want to be)

    Industry can be attractive. I worked about a year in econ consulting and it was interesting. Salaries can be higher than academia—I had an industry offer higher than my academic offers.

    Key point: employers don’t want to be your “second option.” They want serious, smart, hardworking people. How to frame it:

    • I like fast cycles + teamwork + applied impact.
    • I’m excited about solving problems outside academia.

    3. My path: Keystone and Analysis Group

    • Structural estimation (yes 😅)
    • Heavy data work (causal/correlational)
    • Worked with smart economists and PhDs
    • Even wrote a paper

    The bar can be high; problems can be worth millions or billions of dollars.

    4. Econ consulting interviews can feel like a flyout

    • Present your JMP
    • 1-on-1 meetings
    • Questions on models and methods
    • Back-and-forth to test your thinking

    Quant skills transfer if you communicate clearly.

    5. “Quant marketing” isn’t always understood

    It varies by company. For me, some interviewers had quant marketing PhDs—they got my skill set. Elsewhere, translate: what skills you bring, and why you are a good fit.

    6. Data science in tech is different (and harder than ever)

    I did DS interviews and didn’t get offers. The process:

    • Coding screens
    • ML/systems questions
    • Case interviews

    Academic job-talk prep isn’t enough—prep separately.

    7. Connections matter (often more than in academia)

    In industry, many don’t get interviews without referrals. I was lucky to have people introduce me to firms. What to do:

    • 15-min calls with friends and alumni
    • Ask for referrals
    • Ask faculty for intros
    • Be specific about roles

    8. NABE TECH: helpful but random

    NABE TECH can help: resume pool → companies browse → some invite interviews. It worked for me (1 offer), but luck and timing mattered. Lesson: use multiple channels; no single pipeline is reliable.

    Final message If you didn’t get an offer, it hurts. But it’s not a referendum on your ability—you still have options. Whatever you choose: commit seriously. Do your best at every stage. You’re not behind. You’re navigating a noisy market.

    One more data point. Tepper rejected my Ph.D. application in 2016. In 2023, Tepper offered me a faculty job. Life is like a poker game: you can’t control everything. There is luck, and there is your play. Keep playing your hand.

    I have also asked “what about endogeneity?” myself, from the other side of the table. I’m not proud of it.
